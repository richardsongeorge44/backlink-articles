# Liquid Capsule Scale-Up: Why Pilot Settings Fail at Commercial Speed

## The Scale-Up Error Is Treating Settings as Transferable

A liquid fill can produce excellent capsules on a 20-liter development line and fail on a 500-liter production train even when the formula, shell, nominal fill temperature, pump stroke, and line speed appear unchanged. That pattern does not automatically indicate a formulation failure. It often indicates that the team transferred machine settings without transferring the physical conditions those settings were supposed to create.

Scale is not a multiplication of volume. It is a change in heat transfer, fluid movement, pressure, residence time, mechanical loading, environmental exposure, and feedback quality.

An end-to-end [liquid capsule manufacturing guide](https://ahzfsw.com/blog/liquid-capsule-manufacturing) helps map the major unit operations. The harder scale-up question is whether the same critical material state can be maintained at the tank, dosing point, sealing station, and dryer when throughput and hold time change.

A development run proves that the product can be made under one set of conditions. It does not prove the width of the process window. Commercial success depends on controlling that window.

> Commercial scale does not create new physics; it removes the pilot line’s hidden buffer.

## Why a Pilot Run Can Hide a Weak Process Window

Pilot production often looks more stable than it really is because several forgiving conditions operate at once:

- The fill vessel is small enough to reach temperature quickly and remain relatively uniform.
- The transfer path is short, so little heat is lost between the tank and the dosing point.
- The batch is consumed quickly, leaving little time for settling, oxidation, phase separation, or moisture exchange.
- Operators watch the equipment closely and make small corrections before a drift becomes visible in the data.
- Sampling is frequent relative to the batch size, so local problems are more likely to be noticed.
- The line runs below its maximum speed, leaving spare capacity in pumps, dryers, and environmental controls.

Those conditions can conceal the variables that matter most. A technician may raise a tank temperature by a few degrees when the fill thickens, adjust a pump stroke when weights drift, or move capsules to a different dryer position when tackiness appears. The batch passes, but the process has not demonstrated that it can control itself.

At commercial scale, those informal corrections become inconsistent or impossible. A 500-liter vessel may have a temperature gradient. A transfer line may cool the product. One dosing head may foul while the others continue normally. A longer run may allow particles to settle or air to accumulate. The final batch average can still look acceptable even while a specific period, nozzle, or die lane is producing defective capsules.

The central mistake is confusing a successful outcome with a transferable recipe. A temperature display reading 45 degrees C is not the same thing as product reaching the nozzle at 45 degrees C. A mixer turning at 100 rpm is not proof of uniformity. A line producing 30,000 capsules per hour is not necessarily delivering the same fill and seal conditions as a line producing 5,000.

## What Actually Changes When Equipment Gets Larger

### Temperature Is a Location, Not a Setpoint

A larger vessel has a lower surface-area-to-volume ratio. The same jacket setting therefore produces a different heating and cooling profile, with more thermal lag between the vessel wall, the bulk center, the outlet, and the product line.

The temperature probe may read the intended value while the liquid near the outlet remains cooler. A long transfer line can create another temperature drop before the fill reaches the pump or nozzle. A pump, recirculation loop, or high-shear mixer may add heat in one section while another section loses heat to the room.

That difference matters because liquid capsule fills are often rheology-sensitive. A few degrees can change viscosity enough to affect pump pressure, flow response, air release, and delivered mass. Some suspensions and concentrated surfactant systems are also non-Newtonian, meaning viscosity changes with shear rate and the time the product has been under shear. The value measured in a laboratory beaker may not describe the material moving through a commercial transfer line.

For softgels, the gel mass and fill must arrive at the rotary die in a controlled thermal state. Gel temperature affects ribbon formation, thickness, moisture, and sealing behavior. Fill temperature affects viscosity and injection performance. For liquid-filled hard capsules, the most important temperature may be the temperature at the dosing nozzle rather than the temperature recorded in the compounding tank.

A scale-up plan should therefore specify the allowable product temperature at the point of use, the maximum tank-to-nozzle gradient, and the time required for the system to stabilize. The tank setpoint is only one input.

### Mixing Speed Is Not Mixing Performance

The same mixer rpm does not produce the same process in vessels of different size or geometry. Impeller diameter, tip speed, power per unit volume, Reynolds number, vessel baffles, and product viscosity all affect the resulting flow pattern.

A small vessel may have complete turnover with no meaningful dead zones. A larger vessel using the same nominal rpm may contain regions where the active concentration, surfactant distribution, or particle concentration differs from the bulk average. Increasing rpm can solve one problem while creating another by introducing air, damaging fragile particles, or accelerating oxidation.

Suspension fills make the risk obvious. Particles can settle during a long hold, collect near the vessel bottom, or become unevenly distributed in a poorly designed recirculation loop. The total fill mass may remain consistent while the active concentration changes from the beginning to the end of the campaign.

Even a clear, single-phase fill can be affected by scale. Temperature gradients can change local viscosity. High-shear zones can entrain air. A formulation that appears homogeneous by visual inspection may contain bubbles that later produce underfilled capsules or oxidation-related stability problems.

The transferable criterion is not mixer rpm. It is demonstrated homogeneity over the maximum hold time, with acceptable air entrainment and no unacceptable particle-size or phase changes.

### Throughput Changes Thermal and Mechanical History

Higher throughput changes more than the number of capsules produced per hour.

On a softgel line, faster rotary-die operation changes the timing between ribbon casting and encapsulation. It can alter ribbon temperature, moisture distribution, fill injection dynamics, die loading, and the amount of capsule mass entering the drying system per unit of time. A ribbon that seals well at a moderate speed may reach the die with a different thermal and mechanical history at commercial speed.

On a liquid-filled hard capsule line, higher speed increases pump cycling, nozzle demand, cap-placement frequency, and banding throughput. Banding solution may be deposited at the same nominal amount per capsule but have less time to level or dry before the next handling step. A dosing system that is stable at low frequency may show pressure fluctuations, air ingestion, or head-to-head differences at a higher rate.

The machine can display the correct speed while the product experiences a different process. Throughput must therefore be treated as a critical process parameter, not merely a production target.

### Longer Holds Change the Material

The first capsule and the last capsule in a commercial campaign may not have the same history. The last portion of a fill may have spent hours in a vessel, recirculation loop, or heated line. During that time, the material may experience settling, oxidation, moisture uptake, evaporation, phase separation, or a change in viscosity.

Longer holds also increase the opportunity for shell-fill interaction. Warm fills can extract or redistribute shell components, while hygroscopic fills can change shell moisture. In gelatin systems containing reactive ingredients, extended thermal exposure can increase the risk of changes that become visible only during dissolution or stability testing.

Maximum hold time is therefore part of the manufacturing process. It should be established experimentally rather than inferred from the fact that the formula remains visually acceptable.

### Batch Averages Hide Local Failures

A pooled assay or average fill weight can conceal a defect limited to one dosing head, one die lane, one time segment, or one dryer position. This is especially dangerous for potent products, where a small number of underfilled units can matter even if the batch average remains within specification.

Commercial sampling should preserve location and time. Samples should be traceable to the nozzle, die lane, tray position, or production interval that generated them. Otherwise, the analytical result may confirm that the batch average is acceptable without showing whether the process is drifting.

## The Coupled Failure Chain in Liquid Capsules

Liquid capsule manufacturing is a coupled system. The important state variables include product temperature, viscosity, density, particle distribution, entrained air, shell moisture, ribbon thickness, line pressure, pump calibration, banding-solution condition, drying load, and room humidity.

Each state variable can affect several quality attributes at once. That is why isolated adjustments often create new problems.

### A Fill-Weight Problem Can Start as a Heat-Transfer Problem

Fill mass is determined by delivered volume and product density. A nominal pump stroke does not guarantee a nominal mass if the actual displacement, backpressure, viscosity, density, or air content changes.

A pump calibrated with water or with a room-temperature sample may perform differently with a warm, viscous, surfactant-rich fill. Time-pressure systems respond to viscosity and pressure. Piston systems can show changes in displacement or pump slip. Flexible tubing can change behavior with temperature and wear. Entrained air can compress during dosing and expand after the capsule is filled.

For a suspension, a consistent total mass does not guarantee consistent potency. The active concentration can change if particles settle or the recirculation pattern is not uniform.

A gradual downward trend in fill weight may therefore be caused by cooling product, rising viscosity, nozzle fouling, declining vessel level, or settling rather than by an incorrect pump stroke. Changing the stroke without identifying the cause can temporarily restore weight while worsening content uniformity.

### A Seal Problem Can Originate in the Fill or the Environment

Softgel seal integrity depends on ribbon thickness, ribbon moisture, gel temperature, fill temperature, wedge conditions, die alignment, and pressure. A change in fill viscosity can alter injection behavior and the way the fill occupies the cavity. A change in ribbon moisture can produce either a weak seam or excessive softness and deformation.

Liquid-filled hard capsules have a different risk profile, but the same principle applies. The cap must seat correctly, the joint must remain clean, and the banding solution must have the right temperature, viscosity, solids content, coverage, and drying conditions. A band can look complete when wet and later crack as it dries. A band that is too thick may remain soft or trap moisture. A band that is too thin may pass a quick visual check but fail during handling or shipping.

Leakage discovered after drying or packaging is often a delayed expression of an earlier process-state problem. It is not necessarily evidence that the final inspection was inadequate.

### A Dissolution Problem Can Begin During Manufacturing

Drying is not cosmetic finishing. It controls shell moisture, mechanical strength, dimensional stability, and, in some products, the later dissolution profile.

A larger capsule load can change airflow through the capsule bed and create differences between tray edges, tray centers, upper layers, and lower layers. Overdrying can make shells brittle. Underdrying can leave capsules tacky or mechanically weak. Moisture can continue to migrate between fill and shell after the capsules leave the dryer.

Commercial thermal and hold-time history can also affect stability. Depending on the shell and fill, prolonged exposure to heat, oxygen, or reactive excipients may promote shell cross-linking, plasticizer migration, or changes in fill composition. A product can pass initial dissolution testing and fail after storage because the manufacturing history created a less stable starting condition.

That is why final testing cannot compensate for poor control upstream. The process must preserve the right material and shell state before the capsule reaches the laboratory.

## Read the Pattern Before Changing the Setting

The timing and location of a defect often reveal more than the defect label itself.

- **Underfilled capsules appear mainly during startup:** The line may not be primed, the fill may not have reached thermal equilibrium, or air may still be clearing from the dosing path.
- **Fill weight drifts steadily during the run:** Product cooling, vessel-level changes, settling, nozzle fouling, or gradual pressure changes deserve attention before pump-stroke adjustments.
- **One nozzle or dosing head behaves differently:** The likely cause is local calibration, obstruction, tubing condition, valve response, or air ingestion rather than batch-wide formulation failure.
- **Weights alternate high and low between successive units:** Air, inconsistent valve reset, pulsation, or an unstable pressure source may be involved.
- **Leaks cluster in one softgel die lane:** Ribbon thickness, ribbon moisture, die alignment, or local temperature distribution may be uneven across the machine.
- **Leaks appear after drying rather than immediately:** Seal strength, band drying, shell moisture, or post-encapsulation handling may be responsible.
- **Fresh capsules pass dissolution but stability samples fail:** Shell-fill interaction, moisture migration, oxidation, or thermal history may be changing the dosage form over time.

This pattern-based approach prevents a common scale-up mistake: making a global adjustment to correct a local problem. A batch-wide temperature change cannot reliably fix one blocked nozzle or one misaligned die lane.

## Build the Scale-Up Around Controlled States

### 1. Define the Critical Quality Attributes Before Choosing Settings

Start with the product attributes that must remain stable at commercial scale:

- Fill mass and active content uniformity
- Seal integrity and leak rate
- Shell moisture and mechanical strength
- Dissolution or release performance
- Oxidation, degradation, and long-term stability
- Capsule dimensions, appearance, and yield where they affect performance

Then map each attribute to the material state and process condition that control it.

- **Fill mass and content uniformity** depend on viscosity, density, air content, particle distribution, pump calibration, pressure, and product temperature at the dosing point.
- **Seal integrity** depends on ribbon or shell condition, joint alignment, band coverage, sealant properties, temperature, moisture, and drying.
- **Dissolution and stability** depend on shell moisture, fill-shell compatibility, exposure time, thermal history, and the condition of the finished capsule after drying.

This map is more useful than a list of machine setpoints because it shows what must be measured and what can actually cause a failure.

### 2. Characterize the Materials Under Real Operating Conditions

A single viscosity value measured at room temperature is not enough for a fill processed at an elevated temperature. Characterization should cover the conditions the material will experience during mixing, transfer, dosing, and hold.

Useful measurements include:

- Viscosity across the expected temperature range and relevant shear rates
- Density and, where applicable, compressibility or air-release behavior
- Particle-size distribution and settling rate for suspensions
- Homogeneity after the maximum planned hold time
- Thermal and oxidative stability during the longest expected process exposure
- Shell moisture, thickness, flexibility, and mechanical properties
- Banding-solution viscosity, solids content, drying behavior, and compatibility with the shell

For non-Newtonian materials, the shear history used during testing should be recorded. A product may be easy to pump after mixing but become more viscous after standing, or it may thin under shear and recover during the transfer hold.

### 3. Translate Machine Settings Into Scale-Independent Criteria

Scale-up should match functional performance rather than nominal equipment numbers.

For a compounding vessel, the target may be complete homogeneity within a defined time, no unacceptable settling during the maximum hold, no harmful air entrainment, and a controlled temperature gradient. Mixer rpm is only one way to pursue those outcomes.

For a softgel line, useful criteria include:

- Gel temperature and viscosity at the casting point
- Ribbon thickness profile across the full width
- Ribbon moisture at the die
- Fill temperature, pressure, and injection stability
- Seal strength and defect rate by die lane
- Dryer temperature, airflow, load, and finished shell moisture

For a liquid-filled hard capsule line, useful criteria include:

- Product temperature and viscosity at each dosing head
- Head-to-head fill-weight accuracy and precision
- Pump pressure and flow stability
- Cap-body seating and joint cleanliness
- Band width, band mass, continuity, and drying condition
- Leak performance after drying and handling

The objective is not to make the commercial machine display the same rpm, temperature, or strokes per minute as the pilot machine. The objective is to make the product experience equivalent controlled conditions.

### 4. Stage the Transfer and Deliberately Challenge It

An engineering batch using the commercial equipment is more informative than a larger batch on a fundamentally different machine. The engineering run should use production-intent shells, fill materials, pumps, transfer lines, environmental controls, and drying equipment whenever possible.

The transfer should examine more than the middle of a nominal run. Evaluate:

- Startup and stabilization
- Beginning, middle, and end of the batch
- The longest intended hold time
- The low, nominal, and high line speeds within the proposed operating range
- The lowest and highest expected fill viscosity
- Different shell lots or material lots when their variability is meaningful
- Relevant room temperature and humidity conditions

Changing the formulation, equipment, batch size, and operating speed at the same time makes failure diagnosis difficult. Each change removes information about which physical relationship was lost.

### 5. Measure the State at the Point Where It Matters

Final laboratory tests are necessary, but they are too slow to control a rapidly drifting process. Commercial lines need measurements that show what the product is experiencing while it is being made.

Depending on the format and risk, useful controls include:

- Temperature probes in the vessel, outlet, transfer line, and dosing area
- Product pressure near the pump and nozzle
- At-line or inline viscosity checks at the actual processing temperature
- Fill-weight checks identified by nozzle, head, and production interval
- Ribbon thickness and moisture measurements across the softgel web
- Band width, continuity, and drying checks for liquid-filled hard capsules
- Room temperature, relative humidity, dryer airflow, and capsule-bed load
- Automated vision inspection for leaks, deformation, twinning, and band defects

Run charts should show trends by time and location. A pooled average can be useful for release, but it is a poor early-warning system. Alert and action limits should be established before the run and set close enough to the process center to provide time for a controlled response.

The response plan also matters. When a trend appears, the procedure should define whether to stop, adjust, segregate, increase sampling, or investigate. Unrecorded operator corrections may save a batch while destroying the evidence needed to make the process robust.

### 6. Establish Hold Times and Handoff Controls

The process needs defined limits for the time between:

- Completion of compounding and the start of filling
- Filling and sealing or banding
- Encapsulation and initial drying
- Drying and packaging
- Any interruption, recirculation, or equipment restart

Each limit should be supported by data. If recirculation is used to prevent settling, its effect on air entrainment, shear, temperature, and particle size must also be understood. The first and last portions of a batch should be treated as potentially different until the process demonstrates otherwise.

## An Illustrative 20-Liter-to-500-Liter Failure

Consider a liquid-filled hard capsule with a nominal 500-milligram fill. On a 20-liter development system, the fill is held at 45 degrees C and the pump is calibrated using material drawn from the tank. Fill-weight variation remains low during a short run.

On the production line, the 500-liter vessel jacket also reads 45 degrees C. However, the product near the outlet is cooler, and the long transfer line loses additional heat before the liquid reaches the dosing heads. The product arrives at the nozzles several degrees colder than expected. Viscosity rises, pressure changes, and the actual pump response shifts. The run begins with acceptable weights and then trends low as the line remains in the colder state.

If the fill is a suspension, the longer hold creates a second risk. The beginning of the run may contain a different particle concentration from the end because the mixing and recirculation pattern was not proven at commercial scale. A batch-average assay may pass even though the time-based concentration profile is unacceptable.

The tempting correction is to increase the tank temperature or pump stroke. That may improve the average weight while creating an overheated condition during startup, increasing shell exposure or changing the fill’s stability. It also leaves the actual cause unresolved.

A stronger correction follows the physical chain:

1. Measure viscosity and density across the expected temperature range and after the maximum hold.
2. Measure temperature at the vessel outlet, transfer line, and dosing point rather than relying on the jacket probe.
3. Insulate or jacket the transfer path, or use controlled recirculation where appropriate.
4. Calibrate each dosing head with the actual product at its operating temperature and pressure.
5. Define mixing, recirculation, and maximum hold-time requirements for the suspension.
6. Trend fill weight by head and time so startup, mid-run, and end-of-run behavior cannot be hidden by pooling.

The process becomes more robust because it controls the product state at the point of dosing, not because it happens to use the same tank temperature as the pilot batch.

The same logic applies to softgels. If a higher die speed changes ribbon temperature or moisture at the moment of sealing, the corrective action is not simply to repeat the old wedge-temperature setting. Ribbon profile, fill pressure, seal strength, die alignment, and dryer loading must be measured at the commercial operating rate.

## What Commercial Validation Must Prove

Equipment qualification and process validation answer different questions. Qualification shows that the equipment is installed and can operate within its specified limits. Process validation must show that the integrated commercial process consistently produces capsules meeting their critical quality attributes.

A credible validation package should demonstrate:

- Consistency with the actual commercial formulation, shell materials, equipment, and operators
- Acceptable results at the beginning, middle, and end of production
- Comparable performance across dosing heads, die lanes, dryer positions, or other relevant locations
- Control at the intended operating range rather than only at one preferred setpoint
- Performance after the longest planned hold and relevant interruptions
- Detection and response when a monitored state begins to drift
- Stability after commercial drying, packaging, and expected storage exposure

Even when a program uses three performance-qualification batches, three passing batch averages do not automatically demonstrate robustness. If samples are pooled, if only the middle of the run is tested, or if operators quietly correct every drift, the validation has not shown how the process behaves under normal variation.

Batch count matters, but sampling structure and process challenge matter just as much. Robustness is a pattern across time, location, and material variation.

## What to Ask a Manufacturing Partner

A manufacturer’s maximum capsule-per-hour claim says little about scale-up control. A technically mature partner should be able to explain how the commercial line preserves the fill and shell states established during development.

Useful questions include:

- Where is fill temperature measured: in the vessel, at the outlet, or at the dosing point?
- Is pump calibration performed with the actual formulation at operating temperature and pressure?
- How are fill weights trended by nozzle, head, and production interval?
- How are suspension homogeneity and maximum hold time demonstrated?
- For softgels, how are ribbon thickness and moisture controlled across the full web width?
- For liquid-filled hard capsules, how are band width, band continuity, and post-drying leak performance verified?
- Does the engineering batch use the same equipment train and shell supply as commercial production?
- What operating ranges were challenged during scale-up, and which ranges are actually validated?
- What is the documented response when weight, temperature, pressure, or seal trends approach an action limit?

Run charts, sampling plans, scale-up rationale, and deviation history are more revealing than a capacity brochure. A partner that can describe the process window is better positioned than one that only promises to reproduce a nominal recipe.

## Commercial Readiness Means Process Margin

A liquid capsule process is commercially ready when ordinary variation remains inside the critical quality boundaries without constant operator rescue. That requires more than a successful formulation, a capable machine, or a passing final assay.

The process must control the physical state that drives performance: the temperature and viscosity at the nozzle, the uniformity of the fill, the condition of the ribbon or shell, the integrity of the seal, and the moisture history through drying and packaging.

Liquid capsule scale-up succeeds when the product experiences equivalent controlled conditions at commercial throughput. It fails when familiar numbers on the machine display are mistaken for equivalent process behavior.
