# Softgel Stability Starts at the Shell-Fill Interface

## Softgel Stability Starts at the Shell-Fill Interface

The most expensive softgel failures rarely happen on the encapsulation line. They happen later, quietly, after a batch has passed appearance checks, fill-weight testing, and initial disintegration. Six months into stability, capsules that looked perfect may harden, leak, dimple, stick together, or fail dissolution because the shell and fill kept reacting after production ended.

That is the central reality behind softgel development: a softgel is not a passive container. It is a small, sealed chemical system. The gelatin shell, plasticizer, residual water, fill vehicle, active ingredient, impurities, oxygen, and packaging all move toward equilibrium over time. Designing [soft gelatin capsules](https://ahzfsw.com/blog/soft-gelatin-capsules) well means treating that interface as the product’s critical control point.

The rotary die machine forms the capsule. The shell-fill interface determines whether it survives the shelf.

## The Capsule Shell Is a Membrane, Not a Wall

A common beginner mistake is to think of the softgel shell as a flexible bottle. It is closer to a semi-permeable membrane. Small molecules can migrate through it, and the shell’s mechanical properties change as its composition changes.

A typical gelatin softgel shell contains gelatin, water, and a plasticizer such as glycerin or sorbitol. After drying, the finished shell usually holds about 4% to 10% moisture. That residual water is not incidental; it keeps the gelatin network flexible enough to resist cracking. Remove too much water and the shell becomes brittle. Add too much water and it becomes tacky, swollen, or microbially vulnerable.

The fill is just as influential. Oil-based fills, PEG-based fills, suspended actives, botanical extracts, and self-emulsifying systems all create different stresses on the shell. The interface between the two phases becomes a long-term exchange zone where several things can happen:

- **Water migration** from shell to fill, or fill to shell
- **Plasticizer migration** from shell into hydrophilic vehicles
- **Reactive impurity transfer** from fill excipients into gelatin
- **Oxygen-driven degradation** of oils and sensitive actives
- **pH stress** on the gelatin protein network
- **Mechanical weakening** at the seam or along thin shell zones

None of these may show up during the first 24 hours. Many appear only under accelerated or long-term stability conditions. That delayed failure pattern is why a softgel that passes release testing can still be a poor formulation.

## The Most Dangerous Question: Does It Encapsulate?

During early development, teams often ask whether a fill can be encapsulated. That question matters, but it is incomplete. Many fills can be pumped, dosed, sealed, and dried successfully. Far fewer remain stable for 24 months in commercial packaging.

A better question is: **What will this fill do to the shell over time?**

A fill that runs smoothly on the machine may still be unsuitable if it extracts glycerin from the shell, introduces aldehydes, carries peroxide impurities, shifts pH outside the gelatin comfort zone, or changes viscosity during storage. Encapsulation success is a manufacturing event. Stability is a relationship.

A practical development team evaluates both from the beginning.

## Plasticizer Migration Can Turn a Good Capsule Into a Hard One

Plasticizers are added because dried gelatin alone is too brittle for softgel use. Glycerin is the workhorse plasticizer because it is efficient, inexpensive, and highly compatible with gelatin. In oil-filled softgels, it often performs beautifully.

Problems start when the fill has a stronger affinity for glycerin than the shell does. PEG 400 and PEG 600 are common examples. These hydrophilic solvents are useful for dissolving actives that will not dissolve in oils, but they can pull glycerin out of the gelatin shell. As glycerin migrates into the fill, the shell loses flexibility and becomes progressively harder.

That change is not cosmetic. A hardened shell can:

- Crack during bottling or shipping
- Resist rupture during disintegration testing
- Slow dissolution by limiting fill release
- Create consumer complaints about brittle or misshapen capsules
- Produce inconsistent performance across the shelf-life period

Published formulation comparisons have shown dramatic differences between plasticizer systems. Glycerin-plasticized gelatin softgels containing PEG-based fills have been reported to increase in hardness several-fold over months of storage, while sorbitol-sorbitan systems show much smaller changes under comparable conditions.

That does not make glycerin a bad choice. It makes it a context-dependent choice. For fish oil, vitamin E, MCT-based vitamin D, and many oil suspensions, glycerin remains highly practical. For PEG-rich fills, a sorbitol-containing plasticizer blend may be the difference between a stable product and a capsule that slowly turns into a pebble.

## Cross-Linking Is Often Misdiagnosed as a Dissolution Problem

Few softgel defects cause more confusion than gelatin cross-linking. A product may disintegrate normally at release, then fail dissolution months later. The first instinct is often to blame the dissolution method, the gelatin supplier, or the drying cycle. Sometimes those are involved. More often, reactive chemistry is the root cause.

Gelatin contains amino acid residues with reactive functional groups. Aldehydes, peroxides, oxidized oils, certain flavors, some PEG impurities, and degradation products from actives can form chemical bridges between gelatin chains. Over time, these bridges create a tougher, less soluble membrane sometimes called a pellicle.

The capsule may still look acceptable. It may not leak. It may even soften in the dissolution vessel. But the shell does not rupture cleanly, or the pellicle holds the fill back long enough to miss the specification.

Common cross-linking triggers include:

- Aldehyde-containing flavor systems
- Oxidized fish oil or botanical oils
- Peroxide impurities in excipients
- Formaldehyde-releasing packaging components
- Certain preservatives or reactive colorants
- High heat and humidity during storage
- UV exposure in insufficiently protective packaging

The frustrating part is that cross-linking can be highly formulation-specific. One omega-3 softgel may remain stable for two years, while another with a different oil quality, antioxidant system, or capsule colorant develops dissolution delays after three months at 40°C/75% RH.

This is why gelatin compatibility cannot be assumed from dosage form experience alone. Every active, oil lot, solvent system, and shell formula changes the risk profile.

## Oil Fills Are Easier, but Not Risk-Free

Oil-based fills are often considered the natural home of softgel technology. Fish oil, flaxseed oil, evening primrose oil, vitamin E, vitamin D, CoQ10 in oil, and carotenoid suspensions are commercially proven. They avoid many of the plasticizer migration problems seen with PEG fills.

Still, oils bring their own risks.

The biggest is oxidation. Unsaturated oils generate peroxides and secondary oxidation products when exposed to oxygen, light, heat, or trace metals. Those oxidation products can degrade the active, create rancid odors, and contribute to gelatin cross-linking.

Fish oil illustrates the point clearly. A capsule can be well sealed and still fail commercially if the oil going into it has a high peroxide value or anisidine value. Encapsulation slows further oxidation by limiting oxygen exposure, but it does not reverse poor raw material quality. If oxidized oil enters the softgel, the shell simply traps the problem inside.

A robust oil-filled softgel program usually includes:

- Low-peroxide raw oil specifications
- Nitrogen blanketing during mixing and holding
- Antioxidants such as mixed tocopherols where appropriate
- Opaque or amber shells for light-sensitive fills
- Low-oxygen headspace in bulk and finished packaging
- Stability testing that includes odor, assay, peroxide value, and dissolution

Transparent capsules may sell well because consumers like to see the golden fill. That marketing decision has a stability cost when the active is light-sensitive. The best product is often the one where appearance, oxygen control, and photostability are balanced before launch rather than corrected after complaints arrive.

## PEG Fills Demand a Different Development Mindset

PEG-based fills are valuable because they solve solubility problems that oils cannot. Many pharmaceutical actives and specialty nutraceutical ingredients dissolve more readily in PEG 400, PEG 600, propylene glycol, or mixed hydrophilic solvent systems than in triglyceride oils.

The tradeoff is higher shell interaction.

PEG systems can pull water and plasticizer from the gelatin shell. They may contain trace aldehydes or peroxides depending on grade, age, and storage. They can also alter the water activity gradient across the shell. If the fill contains too much free water, the shell may soften. If the fill extracts too much plasticizer, the shell may harden.

For PEG fills, the shell formula should not be treated as standard. Critical choices include:

- Glycerin versus sorbitol-sorbitan plasticizer blends
- Gelatin Bloom strength
- Initial shell moisture target
- Fill water content
- PEG grade and impurity limits
- pH adjustment or buffering strategy
- Antioxidant or cross-linking inhibitor use
- Packaging moisture barrier level

The wrong approach is to develop the fill first, then ask a manufacturer to put it into a standard shell. The shell and fill need to be developed together. Otherwise, the first real compatibility study happens accidentally on the stability shelf.

## Suspensions Add Physical Stability to the Chemical Problem

Not every active dissolves in the fill vehicle. Many softgels contain suspensions: solid active particles dispersed in oil, waxy matrices, or semi-solid systems. These are common for minerals, herbal extracts, carotenoids, and poorly soluble nutraceutical compounds.

Suspensions add another layer of risk because dose uniformity depends on physical stability before and during encapsulation. If particles settle in the holding tank, the first capsules and last capsules in a batch may contain different active levels. If particles are too large, they can interfere with sealing or create weak spots in the shell.

Particle size is especially important. Large or gritty particles can lodge at the seam during rotary die sealing, causing microscopic leak paths. Many softgel suspension programs target particles below roughly 180 to 200 microns, with tighter controls when the capsule is small or the fill contains a high solids load.

Viscosity also matters. A suspension needs enough structure to prevent settling, but not so much that the metering pump loses accuracy. Hydrogenated vegetable oil, beeswax, lecithin, silicon dioxide, and other suspending agents can help, but each changes pumpability and fill release.

The most stable suspension is not always the easiest to encapsulate. The easiest suspension to encapsulate is not always the most stable. The development window sits between those two points.

## pH Limits Are Not Suggestions

Gelatin tolerates a reasonable pH range, but it is not chemically indifferent. Strongly acidic fills can hydrolyze gelatin over time. Alkaline fills can destabilize the protein structure and create softening, discoloration, or leakage.

A practical working range for many gelatin softgel fills is about pH 2.5 to 7.5, although the real risk depends on buffer strength, solvent system, water activity, and storage temperature. A mildly acidic oil suspension behaves differently from a water-rich acidic PEG system.

The most problematic fills are often those with enough water or polar solvent to move reactive species into the shell. A low-pH ingredient dispersed in an anhydrous oil may be manageable. The same ingredient dissolved in a hydrophilic vehicle may attack the shell much more aggressively.

pH should be measured in a meaningful way for the fill system. For nonaqueous fills, direct pH readings can be misleading. Development teams often evaluate apparent pH after extraction into water or use compatibility studies rather than relying on a single meter value.

## Stability Testing Has to Start Before the Pilot Batch

Many softgel failures could be avoided with small, ugly, early compatibility studies. These do not need commercial capsule appearance. They need honest stress conditions.

A useful preformulation program may include:

1. **Binary contact studies**
   
   Place gelatin films or shell pieces in contact with the proposed fill, fill vehicle, active, and key excipients. Store at elevated temperature and humidity. Track swelling, hardening, discoloration, odor, and mass change.

2. **Plasticizer migration screening**
   
   Compare glycerin- and sorbitol-based shell films against the fill. Measure hardness or tensile changes over time. For PEG fills, this screen is often decisive.

3. **Reactive impurity checks**
   
   Test oils for peroxide and anisidine values. Test solvents for aldehydes or peroxide impurities where relevant. Review flavor and color systems for reactive functional groups.

4. **Prototype capsule stability**
   
   Produce a small encapsulation batch and place it immediately into intended packaging and stress packaging. Evaluate disintegration, dissolution, assay, moisture, hardness, leakage, and appearance.

5. **Packaging comparison**
   
   Compare HDPE bottles, blister films, desiccant levels, induction seals, and headspace controls early. Packaging is not separate from formulation; it sets the moisture and oxygen environment for the capsule.

Accelerated stability at 40°C/75% RH is useful, but it can also exaggerate mechanisms that may not dominate at room temperature. Long-term and intermediate conditions remain important. A capsule that survives brutal accelerated storage is reassuring, but a capsule that fails accelerated storage is not always commercially impossible. The failure mechanism has to be understood.

## Desiccants Can Fix One Problem and Create Another

Softgels are sensitive to both excess moisture and over-drying. This makes packaging deceptively difficult.

A desiccant may prevent capsules from sticking in humid climates. It may also pull too much moisture from the gelatin shell, especially in small bottles with high desiccant-to-capsule ratios. Over-dried capsules become brittle, crack under compression, or fracture during shipping.

The right packaging decision depends on the product’s moisture equilibrium. A tropical-market omega-3 softgel may need stronger moisture protection than a product sold in a dry climate. A PEG-filled softgel may require a different strategy because the fill itself influences shell moisture over time.

Good packaging studies track more than appearance. They monitor:

- Shell moisture by Karl Fischer or validated loss-on-drying methods
- Capsule hardness or rupture force
- Leakage after shipping simulation
- Disintegration and dissolution over time
- Fill assay and degradation products
- Odor development for oxidizable oils
- Capsule-to-capsule sticking under high humidity

A bottle that looks premium on the shelf can still be wrong for the formulation.

## Release Testing Is Not Stability Testing

Release specifications confirm that a batch was made correctly. They do not prove that the formulation is stable.

At release, a softgel may meet fill weight, assay, disintegration, microbial limits, and appearance requirements. Those results say little about whether glycerin will migrate into the fill by month six, whether oxidized botanical components will cross-link gelatin by month nine, or whether a desiccant will over-dry the shell by month twelve.

The most informative stability programs include tests that reflect the known failure modes of softgels:

- **Disintegration** to confirm shell rupture
- **Dissolution** to confirm active release, not just shell opening
- **Shell moisture** to detect drying or softening trends
- **Hardness or mechanical strength** to track plasticizer movement
- **Seal integrity** to identify delayed leakage risk
- **Assay and degradation products** to monitor active stability
- **Peroxide or oxidation markers** for oil-based fills
- **Visual inspection** for dimpling, bloom, color shift, and sticking

For gelatin capsules affected by cross-linking, dissolution testing may sometimes be repeated with enzymes under pharmacopeial guidance when justified. That should not become a way to excuse poor formulation. Enzyme-assisted testing helps distinguish an in vitro artifact from a true release failure, but the better solution is to reduce the chemistry that caused cross-linking in the first place.

## Manufacturer Selection Should Focus on Compatibility Experience

Softgel sourcing often starts with questions about price, capacity, capsule size, minimum order quantity, and lead time. Those matter. They do not reveal whether a manufacturer can keep a difficult formulation stable.

A more useful supplier conversation includes questions such as:

- What shell systems are available for oil fills, PEG fills, and suspension fills?
- When is sorbitol-sorbitan preferred over glycerin?
- How are gelatin Bloom strength and viscosity selected?
- What historical problems have appeared with similar fills?
- Are peroxide, aldehyde, pH, and water-content risks screened before encapsulation?
- How are drying endpoints determined: time, moisture, capsule weight, or mechanical behavior?
- Can pilot batches be placed on accelerated and long-term stability before commercial commitment?
- What dissolution method development support is available for liquid-filled capsules?
- How are leakers, twins, dimples, bubbles, and weak seams trended by batch?
- What packaging formats have been validated for comparable products?

The cheapest quote can become expensive if the product fails at month six. Softgels punish superficial sourcing because the most important work happens before scale-up: compatibility screening, shell selection, fill optimization, drying design, and packaging validation.

## The Interface Is the Product

A softgel’s appeal is easy to see: smooth swallowing, precise liquid dosing, strong odor masking, premium appearance, and better performance for many lipophilic actives. Those advantages come from the same feature that creates the risk: intimate, permanent contact between a reactive shell and a reactive fill.

The best softgel programs treat the shell-fill interface as the design center. They choose plasticizers based on fill chemistry, not habit. They test oils for oxidation before encapsulation. They avoid reactive flavors and excipients. They measure moisture movement instead of guessing. They select packaging through stability data rather than shelf aesthetics alone. They view dissolution failure as a chemistry signal, not merely a lab inconvenience.

A softgel is successful when the capsule made on day one is still the same functional dosage form at the end of shelf life. That outcome is not created by the rotary die alone. It is created by controlling the small exchanges happening inside the capsule every day after it leaves the line.
